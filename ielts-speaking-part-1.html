SPEAKING PART 1

Answer familiar questions naturally

Use these original practice questions to build short, clear answers without memorizing scripts.

What Part 1 is testing

Part 1 is built around familiar topics. You should be able to answer directly, communicate clearly and keep a short conversation going. The safest approach is not to turn every response into a long speech. Aim for a clear answer followed by one useful detail.

Useful answer pattern

Direct answer + reason + small example. If the question is “Do you enjoy cooking?”, a natural response could begin with “Yes, especially on weekends,” followed by a reason and a specific dish or situation. The example makes the answer easier to develop.

Home and neighbourhood

  1. What do you like most about the place where you live?
  2. Is there anything you would like to change about your neighbourhood?
  3. Do you know many of your neighbours?
  4. What is the most useful place near your home?
  5. Would you like to live somewhere different in the future?

Free time and hobbies

  1. What do you usually do when you have free time?
  2. Did you have the same hobbies when you were younger?
  3. Do you prefer active or relaxing activities?
  4. Is there a hobby you would like to learn?
  5. How often do you spend time doing something just for fun?

Work and study

  1. What do you like about your work or studies?
  2. What part of your day is usually the busiest?
  3. Do you prefer working alone or with other people?
  4. What skill would you like to improve?
  5. What kind of work would you like to do in the future?

Technology and daily life

  1. What piece of technology do you use most?
  2. Do you often use your phone to learn something?
  3. Has technology changed how you communicate?
  4. Do you prefer reading on a screen or on paper?
  5. Is there any technology you find difficult to use?

How long should your answers be?

There is no benefit in forcing every answer to be the same length. A question that needs only one fact can be answered briefly. For opinion or preference questions, two or three sentences usually give you enough room to explain your idea.

What to avoid

  • One-word answers that end the conversation.
  • Long memorized speeches that do not directly answer the question.
  • Repeated fillers such as “you know” in every sentence.
  • Using advanced words when a simpler word would be more natural.

Try these questions aloud, record yourself, and then repeat them using a different example. That second attempt is where much of the useful practice happens.
